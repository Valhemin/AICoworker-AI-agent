# Screenplay and Scene Writing Playbook

## Writing priority

Write what can be seen, heard, performed, or edited. Avoid prose that describes the author’s intention but gives production no playable behavior.

## Scene entry and exit

Enter after the obvious setup and leave after the turn. Start with the character already pursuing something when possible.

Weak entry: explaining where everyone is and how they feel.
Strong entry: a behavior under pressure that lets the audience infer context.

## Action lines

- present tense;
- concrete nouns and active verbs;
- one visual action or beat per paragraph;
- avoid unfilmable internal explanation unless expressed by behavior, VO, or design;
- note essential continuity, not every physical detail;
- distinguish production instruction from dramatic action.

## Dialogue allocation

Dialogue should perform at least one function:

- pursue an objective;
- conceal or reveal information;
- change power;
- test a relationship;
- create or release tension;
- force a choice;
- reframe what the audience sees.

If dialogue only repeats the image, cut or contrast it.

## Exposition strategies

- make information a weapon, mistake, bargain, obstacle, or source of embarrassment;
- reveal only what changes the current decision;
- distribute context across action and consequence;
- let characters disagree about meaning;
- use props, interfaces, documents, and environment when legible;
- preserve productive questions rather than answering everything early.

## Voice-over

Use VO when it adds a layer that images and dialogue cannot efficiently provide: perspective, compression, irony, memory, argument, or interior contradiction. Avoid narrating every visible action.

## Timing estimate

Spoken language varies by language, performance, and content. Use a scratch read, not only word count. Include pauses, reactions, transitions, and on-screen reading time. For localization, retime after translation or transcreation.

## Draft passes

1. **Purpose pass** — every scene has a function.
2. **Causality pass** — choices create consequences.
3. **Compression pass** — remove repeated information and late starts.
4. **Performance pass** — playable actions and natural speech.
5. **Visual pass** — show what can be shown.
6. **Claim pass** — map factual and product claims.
7. **Platform pass** — duration, orientation, captions, CTA.
8. **Read-aloud pass** — catch stiffness and timing.

## Commercial scripts

Keep product truth separate from dramatic embellishment. Place proof where doubt appears. The product should cause or enable the outcome rather than arrive as an unrelated final logo.

## Educational scripts

Use an observable learning sequence: activate prior knowledge, surface misconception, explain or demonstrate, prompt retrieval/application, then summarize. Entertainment supports learning but cannot replace it.

## Failure patterns

- characters explain their relationship directly;
- every line is polished and quotable;
- all characters share vocabulary and rhythm;
- emotional turning points are narrated rather than played;
- CTA appears without solving the promised problem;
- script depends on shots the model cannot reliably produce.
